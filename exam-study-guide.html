How to Study for Exams

A practical guide to planning your revision, remembering what you learn and preparing for exams without leaving everything until the last minute.

Open the Study Planner

1. Start with a simple plan

You do not need a perfect timetable. You need a realistic one that you can follow.

  1. Write down every subject you need to revise.
  2. Divide each subject into smaller topics.
  3. Mark the topics you find difficult or have not studied yet.
  4. Check how many days you have before each exam.
  5. Give important and difficult topics enough time, and leave room to practise questions.
Remember: Planning is the beginning of revision, not a replacement for it. Start with one manageable topic today.

2. Use active recall

Active recall means trying to remember information without looking at your notes first. It helps you find out what you really know and what needs more work.

Try this method

  1. Study a small section of your notes.
  2. Close your book or hide your notes.
  3. Write down or explain everything you can remember.
  4. Check your notes and correct missing or incorrect information.
  5. Try again later rather than repeating the same section immediately.

For example, after studying a history topic, explain its causes and consequences from memory. For mathematics, attempt a question without looking at the worked example.

3. Spread revision across several days

Instead of trying to learn everything in one long session, return to important topics over time. This is called spaced practice.

One possible schedule for a topic is:

  • Day 1: Learn the topic and test yourself.
  • Day 2: Recall the main ideas and correct mistakes.
  • Day 4: Answer questions without using notes.
  • Day 7: Test yourself again and review weak areas.

This is an example, not a fixed scientific formula. Adjust the intervals to your exam date, progress and difficulty with the topic.

4. Change your method to suit your subject

Mathematics and problem-solving subjects

  • Learn when and why a method is used.
  • Work through an example, then solve a similar question alone.
  • Keep an error log showing what went wrong and how to correct it.
  • Return to difficult question types a few days later.

Essay-based subjects

  • Practise recalling key ideas, definitions, examples and evidence.
  • Make essay plans before writing full answers.
  • Explain how your evidence supports your argument.
  • Practise under timed conditions when you are ready.

Science and content-heavy subjects

  • Draw diagrams from memory and label them.
  • Explain processes in your own words.
  • Use short questions to check facts and understanding.
  • Practise applying ideas to unfamiliar examples.

Languages

  • Review vocabulary regularly and use it in sentences.
  • Practise grammar by answering questions, not just reading rules.
  • Write short responses and check recurring mistakes.
  • Practise reading and writing within your exam's time limits.

6. How to prepare at different educational levels

Secondary school

Break homework and revision into short tasks. Focus on understanding basic ideas before moving to harder questions. Ask a teacher for help when a concept remains unclear.

A-Level and other advanced exams

Check your syllabus, practise exam-style questions and study the requirements of your exam board. For essay subjects, practise developing arguments. For calculation-based subjects, show your working and learn from errors.

University

Organise lecture material throughout the term. Review core concepts, connect ideas across topics, and practise applying knowledge to unfamiliar problems, case studies or essay questions.

7. What if your exam is close?

If you have limited time, do not try to rewrite every page of your notes.

  1. Check the syllabus or course outline.
  2. Identify important topics you do not understand well.
  3. Review the core ideas, then answer practice questions.
  4. Use mistakes to decide what to revise next.
  5. Leave time for sleep, meals and breaks.

You cannot guarantee a particular result by following a timetable. However, a realistic plan helps you use the time you have more purposefully.

8. Your revision checklist

Before finishing a study session, ask yourself:

  • Can I explain the main idea without looking at my notes?
  • Have I answered at least one question or completed a useful practice task?
  • Have I checked and corrected my mistakes?
  • Do I know what I need to revisit next time?
